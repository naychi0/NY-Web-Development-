About Myanmar Tea

We want to share Myanmar’s beautiful tea culture with the world.

Myanmar is one of the few places in the world where tea is both drunk as a beverage and eaten as food. A strong, creamy cup of pulled milk tea in the morning, a plate of laphet thoke in the afternoon – both start from the same leaf.

Corner teahouses are the social heart of every town. Friends, families and neighbours gather over tea, fritters and sweet pastries to talk, rest and forget about the bustling day.

Laphet, the fermented tea leaf, was historically an ancient peace offering to settle disputes. Today it remains a symbol of hospitality, served at weddings, ceremonies and festive gatherings.

Myanmar Tea Culture

1. Drinking Culture

Burmese milk tea (လက်ဖက်ရည် · laphet yay) is the heart of every teahouse.

Read more
  • The Brew: unlike spiced Indian chai or sweet Thai tea, it uses strongly brewed black tea with evaporated milk and condensed milk.
  • The Pulling Technique: vendors “pull” the tea by tossing or stretching the liquid between containers to cool it and create a rich, frothy foam.
  • Custom Ratios: teahouses take orders with shorthand terms for how sweet, strong or creamy you want it – standard, rich, or bitter/astringent.
  • Teahouse Life: corner teahouses are vital social hubs, with fritters and sweet pastries (မုန့် – mont).
  • Clear Tea (ရေနွေးကြမ်း): most restaurants and homes serve a complimentary pot of hot, plain tea or water.

2. Eating Culture

Fermented tea leaves (လက်ဖက် · laphet) – tea you can eat.

Read more
  • National Delicacy: juvenile tea leaves are steamed, then fermented in clay pots or bamboo vats for months.
  • Tea Leaf Salad (လက်ဖက်သုပ်): tossed with toasted peanuts, roasted sesame, fried garlic, dried shrimp, sliced chilis, lime and peanut oil.
  • Cultural Significance: an ancient peace offering and a symbol of hospitality at weddings, ceremonies and festive gatherings.

3. Production & Origins

From the cool hills of Shan State to your cup.

Read more
  • Regions: indigenous tea cultivation thrives in the cool, high-altitude hills of Shan State and northern regions, grown by ethnic groups like the Shan and Palaung.
  • Varieties: beyond local green and black teas, Myanmar produces specialty styles like raw pu-erh from ancient, centuries-old trees.
